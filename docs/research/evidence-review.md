# Keep the method, grow the content

The app's method is close to what the research recommends; its main shortfall is the amount of material, not the design. The practices with the strongest evidence for adults are already built in: spaced review (FSRS at 90% retention), typed recall, short explicit grammar followed by practice that comes back in review, comparing your writing with a model and logging mistakes, English glosses inside the text, and reading the questions before listening. The gaps are mostly volume. 1,450 words is about 60% of the Goethe B1 list (about 2,400 items) and a quarter to a third of the 4,000–6,000 words linked to C1. And 26 texts plus 24 scripts cannot supply the reading volume that incidental learning needs, because learners pick up only 9–18% of new words from a text. The best next steps are cheap: send looked-up glosses into review, guide the writing comparison towards grammar and add a rewrite, give new words several retrievals on first meeting, and test noun plurals. The expensive ones are growing the word bank in frequency order and adding input in topic series. Two caveats apply throughout. Nearly all the evidence comes from learners of English, often Japanese university students. Several German-specific choices (gender, plurals, separable verbs) rest on general principles, not trials.

## Spacing and retrieval have strong evidence; much of the rest is thin

Evidence quality varies a lot, so the table below grades it. "Strong" means several meta-analyses agree (a meta-analysis pools many studies). "Moderate" means one meta-analysis or several consistent studies. "Weak" means a single study, small studies, or theory.

| Principle | Strength | Key figure |
|---|---|---|
| Spaced practice beats massed practice | Strong | g = 0.58 immediate, 0.80 delayed (37 L2 studies) |
| Practice tests beat restudy | Strong | 118 articles; mixed formats best |
| Explicit grammar beats implicit | Strong, with a caveat | d = 1.13 vs 0.54 |
| Written corrective feedback works and lasts | Strong | 52 studies, moderate durable effect |
| L1 glosses aid incidental vocabulary | Strong | 45% vs 27% of words learned |
| Interleaving helps similar categories, not word lists | Strong (not L2-specific) | g = 0.42 overall; words g = −0.39 |
| Extensive reading improves rate and comprehension | Moderate–strong | rate d = 0.98, vocabulary d = 0.18 |
| Progress records and if-then plans aid persistence | Strong (not L2-specific) | d = 0.40 and d = 0.65 |
| Typed recall beats "think, then flip" for complex items | Moderate | d = 0.43 |
| Production practice gives more durable productive gains | Moderate | meta-analysis, delayed tests |
| About 5 retrievals at first meeting | Weak | one study |
| Model-text comparison improves writing | Weak–moderate | small studies, mostly vocabulary noticed |
| Gender: colour coding helps | Not supported | one study, no benefit over article + noun |
| TTS is as good as a human voice | Weak | one study of 29 learners |

### Vocabulary: spacing, typed recall and frequency

Spacing is the best-supported factor in L2 vocabulary learning. Kim and Webb's meta-analysis of 37 studies found **g = 0.58 on immediate tests and g = 0.80 on delayed tests** ([Abdurahmonov & Kim 2026](https://journal.kci.go.kr/meeso/archive/articlePdf?artiId=ART003314179)). Equal and expanding gaps did equally well ([Kim & Webb summary](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/49487)). The best gap grows with how long you need to remember: about 20–40% of a one-week horizon, and **5–10% of a one-year horizon**. Gaps that are too short hurt more than gaps that are too long ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)). FSRS predicts forgetting better than older schedulers in a benchmark of **10,000 Anki users and about 727 million reviews**. But that benchmark measures prediction, not learning, and a plain moving-average baseline comes close to FSRS-6 ([srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)). The claim that FSRS needs 30–50% fewer reviews than SM-2 comes from its own developer ([L-M-Sherlock](https://l-m-sherlock.notion.site/What-are-the-main-differences-between-SM-2-and-FSRS-135c250163a180ca9029ea460136254c)). In short, the scheduler matters less than content and retrieval format.

How you retrieve matters. For simple word pairs, recalling silently and then flipping works as well as typing ([Smith et al. 2013](http://psychnet.wustl.edu/memory/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Smith-et-al-2013_JEPLMC.pdf)). For more complex targets, typed or spoken recall wins (**pooled d = 0.43**), because silent recall lets you stop at "feels familiar" ([Tauber et al. 2018](https://www.adamlputnam.com/uploads/8/3/5/6/83563830/tauber_et_al._2017.pdf)). A German noun with its article and plural is a complex target. Learners also misjudge their own learning. After massed practice they predicted the same retention as after spaced practice, yet one week later spaced items were recalled **1.6 times as often** ([Nakata & Suzuki 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-massing-and-spacing-on-the-learning-of-semantically-related-and-unrelated-words/F58BA8D70385603B9C42E408BFCB8A10)). Students who flashcarded alone rated their learning about **20% above** their test scores ([Metacognition and Learning 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11409-024-09406-w)). So grades should come from correctness, not feelings.

Direction matters, and practice in one direction transfers only partly to the other. A small study (n = 28) found English→L2 retrieval worked better for stronger learners and L2→English for weaker ones ([Terai et al. 2021](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/effects-of-learning-direction-in-retrieval-practice-on-efl-vocabulary-learning/159EE50F4B8835207764FB1B11077F29)). With no speaking, typed production is the only route to productive vocabulary. Context is complementary, not superior: a glossed sentence added little to word-pair learning of form and meaning ([Webb 2007](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362168806072463)). Sentences teach how a word behaves, such as *warten auf* + accusative. One single study found that **5 or 7 retrievals in the first session beat 1 or 3** ([Nakata 2017](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/does-repeated-practice-make-perfect-the-effects-of-withinsession-repeated-retrieval-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/F14BA8A576CD2563D14CEA46E35D842E)). Teaching close sets together (*Mieter/Vermieter*, *legen/liegen*) did not lower recall overall, but it caused more mix-ups between them (Nakata & Suzuki 2019, above).

Size targets are rough and come mostly from English. One scale puts **B1 at 2,500–3,250 and C1 at 3,750–4,500** of the 5,000 most frequent lemmas (a lemma is a dictionary headword). Nation sets C1 at 5,000–6,000 word families ([Nation](https://wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/vocabulary-lists/vocabulary-cefr-and-word-family-size/vocabulary-and-the-cefr-docx)). The Goethe B1 list has about 2,400 items ([Goethe B1 Wortliste](https://www.goethe.de/pro/relaunch/prf/da/Goethe-Zertifikat_B1_Wortliste.pdf)). The 4,034 headwords of the Jones and Tschirner frequency dictionary cover only **80–90% of running German text** ([Frequency Dictionary of German](https://esakademi.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/RANDAL1.pdf)). Independent reading needs about 98% ([Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski, via JALT](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/37.4tlt_art1.pdf)). Gender is a major burden for adults learning German. In the largest study (n = 283), colour coding did no better than learning the article with the noun. Images that showed both meaning and gender did best, but that is one study ([ERIC EJ1065542](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1065542)). I found no intervention studies on plurals, separable verbs or strong verbs.

### Reading and listening: volume, glosses and questions first

Incidental learning is real but slow. A meta-analysis of 24 studies found learners picked up only **9–18% of target words immediately and 6–17% later** ([Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa 2023](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C)). In one graded reader, no word met fewer than **8 times** was remembered three months later ([Waring & Takaki 2003](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/1626146/Waring-Takaki-2003.pdf)). Extensive reading mainly builds speed and comprehension: **d = 0.98 for reading rate, 0.63 for comprehension and 0.18 for vocabulary** ([Nakanishi 2015, via Frontiers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188915)). Adults gain more than children (d = 0.70, same source). Comprehension rises steeply with the share of words you know: **95% is the minimum with support, 98% for independent reading** ([Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski 2010](https://ritsumei.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/16831/files/3.pdf)).

Glosses help. Across 42 studies, glossed reading taught **45.3% of target words immediately versus 26.6%** without glosses ([Yanagisawa, Webb & Uchihara 2020](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922)). English glosses beat German ones at every level. Multiple-choice glosses, where you guess the meaning before it is revealed, worked best. End-of-text glossaries worked worst. Picture or audio glosses added nothing over text. Reading topic series ("narrow reading") recycles words and fits the 8-meetings finding. But I found no controlled comparison, so this rests on mechanism, not trials ([Chang 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/effects-of-narrow-reading-and-listening-on-l2-vocabulary-learning/636D23C4D711F7E93779D98BF7F16F8B)).

For listening, previewing the questions had the strongest effect of four supports tested. Repetition helped stronger listeners more than weaker ones ([Chang & Read 2006, via SOAR](https://soar-ir.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2283/files/EducationR06-02.pdf)). A taught cycle of predicting, listening, checking and reflecting beat a control group, mostly for weaker listeners (106 learners of French, approximately replicated) ([Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari 2010](https://agpci.dlsu.edu.ph/agpci/article.php?a=22472)). Modern TTS was about as comprehensible as a human voice in one study of 29 learners, though less natural ([Bione & Cardoso 2020](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/944/)). Perception training across 79 studies gave **g = 0.67**, moderated by the number of different speakers ([HVPT meta-analysis 2025](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/6ABB8C1F32D88D53EA8D05A4565E76F6/S0272263125100879a.pdf/high-variability-phonetic-training-hvpt-a-meta-analysis-of-l2-perceptual-training-studies.pdf)). Nothing tests whether TTS-only listening prepares you for real speech, which is full of reductions and fillers that TTS lacks.

### Grammar and writing: explicit teaching, production, guided feedback

Explicit instruction beat implicit instruction across 77 studies: **d = 1.13 versus 0.54** ([Norris & Ortega, via Ke et al. 2021](https://testplatform.openjournals.nl/dujal/article/download/9361/12389/22029)). The caveat matters. Most of those studies were short and used tests that favour rule knowledge. Longer studies that measured free writing found no explicit advantage (Ke et al., same source). So exercise scores overstate what will show up in your own writing. Production practice gave better delayed results on production tests than input practice ([Shintani, Li & Ellis 2013, via CASLS](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/23350)). Mixing grammar structures in practice improved 7-day retention in one study, but replications are mixed. Shorter gaps (about 1–4 days) were as good or better for accurate production early on ([Serrano 2022](https://dx.doi.org/10.14746/ssllt.2022.12.3.2)). Across subjects, mixing helps most when categories are easily confused (**g = 0.42**) and hurts word-list learning (**g = −0.39**) ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)). That points to mixing confusable pairs like dative and accusative, not shuffling everything.

German word order develops in a fixed sequence. The predicted order fitted **100%** in 21 tutored English-speaking learners ([ANU](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/acquisition-of-german-word-order-in-tutored-learners-a-cross-sect/)). Expect verb-second and verb-final errors in free writing long after you pass the gap-fills. Intermediate adult learners of German relied on word order and meaning rather than case endings to understand sentences ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-use-and-non-use-of-semantic-information-word-order-and-case-m/)). Case needs comprehension practice, not only production.

Written corrective feedback has **moderate, durable effects across 52 studies**, with direct, indirect and rule-based feedback roughly equal ([Brown, Liu & Norouzian 2023](https://todoele.net/node/271041)). Lower-level learners gain more from being shown the correct form ([Kang & Han 2015, via CASLS](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/24045)). Without a teacher, the nearest researched substitute is comparing your draft with a model. Learners mostly notice vocabulary that way: **60.6% of what they noticed was lexical**. Guided prompts that tell them what to check raised what they noticed, and gains held after a week ([Wu, Qie & Wang 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1156553/pdf)). Models pitched well above the learner's level went unused (same source). The classic procedure is: note problems while writing, compare with the model, rewrite, and rewrite again later. Sentence combining is among the effective writing practices in a meta-analysis of first-language writers ([Graham & Perin 2007](https://www.adlit.org/topics/writing/summary-writing-next)). L2 evidence is only small studies. Dictogloss (hear a short text, then reconstruct it in writing) has weak, mixed evidence.

### Motivation, time and exams

Rewards for simply doing a session reduce intrinsic motivation (**d = −0.40** for engagement-based rewards across 128 studies) ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)). Gamification's learning effect (g = 0.49) holds up, but its motivation effect was unstable in rigorous studies ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)). Two levers without games have stronger support. Prompting people to monitor progress raised goal attainment (**d = 0.40**), more when progress was recorded ([Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)). If-then plans ("when I finish breakfast, I do my reviews") gave **d = 0.65** ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)). Neither comes from language learning. Self-assessment tracks real proficiency only loosely (**r ≈ .45**) and works better when tied to specific tasks ([Li & Zhang 2021, via Language Teaching](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/786D4E0E0D500DCDD43CCE24ED610592/core-reader)).

Goethe estimates **350–650 teaching units of 45 minutes for B1 and 800–1,000 for C1**, cumulative ([Goethe-Institut London FAQ](https://goethe.de/ins/gb/en/sta/lon/kur/faq.html)). Another summary of Goethe pages gives lower figures, so treat these as rough. A2 to C1 is several hundred more hours, and these are classroom hours, not app hours. Nation's "four strands" give roughly equal time to input, output, focused study and fluency ([TESL Ontario](https://contact.teslontario.org/?p=2244)). That is a design principle, not a tested ratio. Practice tests beat restudy, and mixed formats worked best ([Adesope et al. 2017, via Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/2/9-1)). The Goethe B1 has four separately passable modules. Lesen and Hören have 30 items each, and **18 correct answers pass** ([Goethe B1 rules](https://www.unifr.ch/centredelangues/en/assets/public/documents/Goethe/DFB_Sept25_DE_EN.pdf)). Lesen has five parts in 65 minutes, and Hören parts 2 and 3 are played only once ([Goethe B1 Übungssatz](https://www.goethe.de/pro/relaunch/prf/materialien/B1/B1_Uebungssatz_Erwachsene.pdf)). Speed-reading courses on easy texts reliably raise reading rate in small replicated studies ([Chung 2010](https://cup.cuhk.edu.hk/chinesepress/journal/AJELT20/AJELT20_95-116.pdf)). Rate matters for the exam: 30 items in 65 minutes.

## The app already follows most of the high-evidence practices

The vocabulary core matches the evidence closely. FSRS at 90% retention with fuzz lets gaps grow; 90% is a sensible default, though no study sets the best figure. FSRS is expanding, but equal gaps work as well, so the schedule shape doesn't matter. Production cards require typing, nouns must include the article, and the checker is lenient: umlaut spellings, ß/ss, capitals, commas and small typos come back as "almost", not "wrong". That is the overt, detailed retrieval that German needs. Recognise and produce are separate cards, which matches the limited transfer between directions. Alternating production with cloze adds the usage knowledge that isolated pairs miss. Taking the suggested grade from typed correctness guards against overconfident self-grading. Irregular verb forms as a separate card fit the advice to stagger principal parts. Capping sessions at 60 reviews and requeuing failures keeps reviews spaced rather than crammed.

Grammar follows the best-supported model: a short explicit explanation, common mistakes, then exercises that return through spaced review. Mixing grammar cards into the shared queue provides the interleaving that helps with confusable rules. Word-order and transformation exercises supply production practice, which gave the more durable results.

Reading uses English glosses attached to words in the text, the form the meta-analysis favours over an end glossary. True/false, multiple-choice and open questions with model answers cover both checking and output. The ad-matching tasks reproduce Lesen Teil 3: 7 situations, 9 usable ads and one "0". Listening already does what the evidence suggests. It tells you to read the questions first and listen without the transcript, then reveals the transcript afterwards. It uses more than one voice and has a speed setting. Dictation adds objective, self-markable form practice.

Writing is the strongest match. Content points act as concrete product goals. Model answers allow self-comparison, a researched form of feedback. Logged mistakes return as cards that show your own wrong sentence to correct. That is focused, output-based, spaced feedback, which is what the corrective-feedback evidence implies. The timed exam writing simulation is useful exam practice.

The motivation design is defensible. Leaving out streaks, XP and leaderboards avoids the rewards most linked to lower intrinsic motivation. Progress stats and weekly input and output minutes on the Today page are recorded progress monitoring, and they nudge you towards balance across Nation's strands.

## Gaps: volume, missing links between parts, German form details

**Vocabulary volume and selection.** 1,450 words falls short of the ~2,400-item B1 list and far short of the 4,000–6,000 words linked to C1. The words are chosen by theme, not ranked by frequency, so some common words may come late. Thematic files also put near-pairs together (*einziehen/umziehen/ausziehen*, *Mieter/Vermieter*), which raises confusion errors.

**Input volume.** Fifty texts and scripts are far below what incidental learning needs (8+ meetings per word, many texts). Texts are not graded against the words you know, so there is no way to choose 95–98% coverage texts. Nothing points you to outside input, which is where most C1-level volume will have to come from.

**Glosses do not feed review.** As far as the code shows, tapping a gloss shows the meaning and stops there. The research suggests two things it doesn't do: let you guess the meaning before it is revealed, and add looked-up words to review.

**First exposure is thin.** The Learn quiz requeues wrong words until each is answered correctly once. More same-day retrievals depend on FSRS learning steps and on you returning to Review. That is well short of the 5 retrievals in Nakata (2017), though that is a single study. The daily new-word number is fixed, not adjusted to how many reviews are due.

**German form details.** Plurals are stored but not tested on production cards. Article errors and noun errors are not scored separately, so the scheduler can't tell which part failed. Case is practised mainly as production, not as comprehension of sentences with a fronted object.

**Writing comparison is unguided.** Without prompts, comparison mostly catches vocabulary. There is no "what I couldn't say" note before the model is shown, no rewrite step, and no later rewrite. The app also doesn't measure errors per 100 words in your writing, which is the only accuracy measure that reflects real use.

**Listening realism.** All audio is TTS from three voices with scripted speech. There is no human, unscripted audio and no play-once mode for Hören parts 2 and 3.

**Exam coverage.** Lesen Teile 1, 2, 4 and 5 are not reproduced as timed exam sets, matching has no timer, and no score shows a projected 0–100 against the 60 pass line.

**Fluency and planning.** The speed round covers word recall only. There is no timed easy reading with a words-per-minute log, no if-then study plan, and no comparison of your predicted score with your actual one.

## Twelve improvements, cheapest strong wins first

Ranking weighs benefit to an A2 learner heading for B1, evidence strength and effort. Small means a day or two of work, medium about a week, large several weeks, mostly content creation.

| Rank | Change | Effort | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Add an "add to review" action to glosses, and add looked-up words to review automatically; for target words, offer a guess-first, multiple-choice gloss | Small–medium | Strong (glossing meta-analysis; spacing) |
| 2 | Guided writing cycle: a "couldn't say" note before the model, a checklist tied to the task's grammar (verb second, verb final after *weil/dass*, case after prepositions, adjective endings), a rewrite step, and a rewrite card about two weeks later | Small | Moderate (guided noticing; corrective feedback); weak for self-study specifically |
| 3 | Grow the word bank to the full Goethe B1 list, introduced in frequency order, then frequency-ranked B2–C1 words towards 4,000–5,000 | Large | Moderate (size–level links; coverage studies), mostly English data |
| 4 | Exam sets for Lesen Teile 1, 2, 4 and 5 and Hören play-once, with an optional timer and scores shown out of 100 against the 60 line | Medium | Strong for practice testing; weak for format familiarity alone |
| 5 | Three to five spaced retrievals per new word on the Learn page; spread near-pairs over different days; pause new words when due reviews pass a threshold | Small | Weak (single study; no direct evidence on daily load) |
| 6 | If-then study plan on Today; "studied 5 of the last 7 days" without resets; predicted-versus-actual score on exam tasks; a one-line note that spaced and mixed practice feels harder but works | Small | Strong for planning and monitoring in general; not L2-specific |
| 7 | Test the plural on noun production cards (e.g. a second field) and score the article separately | Small | Weak (follows from typed-recall evidence; no German trials) |
| 8 | Grammar items that need meaning to answer, case-comprehension items (*Den Hund sieht der Mann*: who sees whom?), and sentence-combining tasks for subordinate clauses; track logged errors per 100 words by topic | Medium | Moderate (production practice; L1 sentence combining); case finding is one study |
| 9 | Show each text's share of known words, add texts in topic series that reuse vocabulary, and link outside learner audio and reading from B1 onwards | Large | Moderate (extensive reading and coverage); weak for topic series |
| 10 | Weekly timed easy reading in fluency mode (no glosses) with a words-per-minute chart | Small–medium | Weak–moderate (small replicated studies) |
| 11 | More listening variety: extra voices, scripts with spoken features (*halt, mal, eben*, contractions), then human audio from B1 | Medium | Weak–moderate (speaker-variety training; small TTS studies) |
| 12 | Train FSRS parameters on your own review log after a few thousand reviews | Medium | Weak for learning gains (prediction only) |

Items 1, 2 and 5–7 are all small and together close most of the design gaps. Item 3 is the single biggest gap for reaching B1 and beyond, but your B1 course will cover part of that vocabulary. Importing course words through the custom-word feature is a cheap bridge until the bank grows. Item 9 matters most after B1. By then most new vocabulary has to come from reading and listening, and the app cannot hold enough material itself, so pointing outwards is cheaper than writing more texts.

Several things are not worth building. Colour-coding genders did no better than the article alone. German-German glosses did worse than English ones at every level. Picture or audio glosses added nothing over text. Streaks and XP raise usage but carry the reward-type motivation cost. Heavily slowed audio has no reliable support; modest slowing is enough. Typed answers on recognition cards are fine but optional, because silent recall matches typing for simple pairs. A quick-flip option would save time without loss.

## Conclusion

The research gives this app's method a good score and its content a weak one. That is the opposite of most commercial apps, which tend to have plenty of content and weak method. The method choices that are hard to change later are already right: typed retrieval, spaced review across every skill, and feedback from your own errors. The remaining work is mostly connecting what exists and adding volume. Glosses should feed review, writing should loop back to grammar, and the word bank should follow frequency.

A second point follows from the evidence on explicit knowledge. Exercise scores will flatter you. The test that matters for B1 writing, and later for C1, is the error rate in your own texts. Measuring it per grammar topic would tell you when a topic is learned, as opposed to merely practised. Without speaking practice, your reading and listening will run ahead of your writing. Taking the Goethe modules separately, which the rules allow, fits that uneven profile.
