# Grammar instruction, practice, corrective feedback and writing development (adult L2, self-study German app)

Context: A2 learner moving to B1, aiming for C1. The app has grammar topics (explanation, common mistakes, gap-fill / multiple choice / word order / transformation exercises feeding a spaced-repetition queue) and writing tasks (content points, useful phrases, model answer, learner self-compares and logs own mistakes as review cards). No AI or server marking. No speaking.

Effect-size yardstick used below: Plonsky & Oswald's L2-specific benchmarks for between-group d are small ≥ 0.40, medium ≥ 0.60, large ≥ 0.90. Cohen's general benchmarks underestimate typical L2 effects — [Plonsky & Oswald 2014, via NAU / search summary](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/how-big-is-big-interpreting-effect-sizes-in-l2-research/)

Method note: many full texts were paywalled. Several frequently quoted numbers (e.g. Goo et al. 2015 g values, Li 2010 overall d, Lyster & Saito 2010 d, Kang & Han 2015 g) could not be verified in this session. They are listed under Gaps, not as findings.

## 1. Explicit vs implicit instruction, and whether explicit knowledge becomes usable

### Takeaway
Three meta-analyses agree that explicit instruction (rules explained, attention drawn to form) beats implicit instruction, with large effects for explicit (Norris & Ortega: d = 1.13 vs 0.54). The advantage holds on delayed tests and on free-production measures in Spada & Tomita. The main caveat: most studies are short and use tests that favour explicit knowledge. Long classroom studies with free writing show smaller or no differences.

### Cited Findings
- Norris & Ortega (2000), 77 research reports: explicit instruction d = 1.13 vs implicit d = 0.54; non-overlapping confidence intervals. Authors warned of bias from test types favouring explicit knowledge, brief treatments and method variation — [Ke, Luo, Piggott & Steinkrauss 2021, Dutch J. Applied Linguistics (summarising N&O)](https://testplatform.openjournals.nl/dujal/article/download/9361/12389/22029)
- Norris & Ortega's four conclusions: instruction beats mere exposure; Focus on Form and Focus on FormS are about equally effective; explicit beats implicit; instructional effects are durable — [Columbia SALT working paper summary](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/download/1534/584/3837); [COERLL Foreign Language Teaching Methods, Grammar module](https://coerll.utexas.edu/methods/modules/grammar/02)
- Spada & Tomita (2010), 30 studies: explicit instruction produced larger effects than implicit for both simple and complex features. The largest effect was explicit instruction of complex features measured by free constructed-response tasks (a proxy for implicit/usable knowledge). Explicit instruction also held up better on delayed post-tests — [CASLS InterCom summary](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/23177); [U Toronto listing](https://www.eas.utoronto.ca/node/790)
- Goo, Granena, Yilmaz & Novella (2015): 34 samples (11 from Norris & Ortega, 23 new, 1999–2011); explicit instruction again more effective than implicit — [De Gruyter / Benjamins abstract](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/sibil.48.18goo/html); [search summary of same](https://lwc1.benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.48.18goo)
- Caveat: the Spada & Tomita and Goo et al. samples share N&O's problems (measures favour explicit knowledge; treatments brief). Studies using free-response data over long periods (Macaro & Masterman 2006; Piggott 2019; Rousse-Malpat et al. 2019) did not find explicit instruction superior — [Ke et al. 2021](https://testplatform.openjournals.nl/dujal/article/download/9361/12389/22029)
- Single study (Ke et al. 2021; 114 Dutch secondary EFL learners, two school years): explicit and implicit instruction equally effective for overall writing quality, complexity and fluency. Explicit group better at correct verb *forms*; implicit group better at choosing the right tense/aspect in context — [Ke et al. 2021](https://testplatform.openjournals.nl/dujal/article/download/9361/12389/22029)
- Interface position (DeKeyser): skill acquisition theory holds that declarative (explicit) knowledge can be proceduralised and then automatised through practice. DeKeyser argues both input and output practice are needed, each building the matching skill (comprehension vs production); VanPatten favours input practice — [summary of VanPatten 2003 vs DeKeyser 2007 positions](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/n/article/view/18876); [DeKeyser & Criado 2013, "Practice in Second Language Instruction" (PDF, not fetchable)](https://digitum.um.es/digitum/bitstream/10201/142082/1/DeKeyser%20and%20Criado_Practice%20in%20Second%20Language%20Instruction_2013.pdf)

### Inferences
- The app's design (short explicit explanation, then practice) matches the best-supported approach for adults. Keep explanations short. The benefit comes from explanation *plus* practice, not from reading rules.
- Gains on controlled tests (gap-fill, multiple choice) overstate gains in free writing. The app should also track accuracy in the learner's own writing (e.g. count logged errors per 100 words by topic). Exercise scores alone will mislead.
- Explicit teaching appears to help most with form (endings, verb forms). Choosing the right form in context (e.g. Perfekt vs Präteritum, Konjunktiv II use) needs meaningful input and use. Add meaning-focused items and reading, not just more drills.

### Gaps
- Exact Goo et al. (2015) and Spada & Tomita (2010) effect sizes were not verifiable here (commonly quoted: Goo et al. explicit g ≈ 1.29, implicit g ≈ 0.77; treat as unverified).
- No meta-analysis found specific to German as a foreign language (DaF) on explicit vs implicit instruction.
- Direct evidence that explicit knowledge "converts" to implicit knowledge remains contested. Measures of implicit knowledge are themselves debated. No decisive source located this session.

## 2. Which practice types build accurate, fluent use

### Takeaway
Both input-based and output-based instruction work, with large effects. Output practice gives better *delayed* productive results. That matters for a writing-focused app. Mechanical drills alone are contested. Spacing reliably helps, but longer gaps are not always better for grammar. Interleaving (mixing) exercise types improved delayed retention in one controlled grammar study, with mixed replications.

### Cited Findings
- Shintani, Li & Ellis (2013), meta-analysis of comparative studies, Language Learning 63(2): both comprehension-based instruction (CBI) and production-based instruction (PBI) had large effects on receptive and productive tests. CBI was better on immediate receptive tests. Both were effective on immediate productive tests. PBI was better on delayed productive tests — [Kansai University publication list / ELT Research Bites summary](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/23350)
- Secondary synthesis: input-based instruction worked better for features in a single phrase (basic morphology, vocabulary). Output-based worked better for complex phrases and clauses with a syntactic component (e.g. relative clauses, conditionals) — [search summary citing Shintani's meta-analytic work](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/23350). Shintani (2015) also published a processing-instruction meta-analysis (Applied Linguistics 36(3), 306–325) — [Shintani publications](https://www2.kansai-u.ac.jp/shintani/publication/index.html)
- Wong & VanPatten (2003), "The evidence is IN: Drills are OUT": a review arguing that mechanical drills (form manipulation without attention to meaning) are neither necessary nor beneficial for acquisition or fluency. Uses Paulston's (1972) distinction between mechanical, meaningful (meaning matters, content known) and communicative drills (new information exchanged). This is a position review, not a meta-analysis — [Wong & VanPatten abstract (PDX)](https://web.pdx.edu/~fischerw/courses/advanced/methods_docs/pdf_doc/wbf_collection/0351_0400/0385abs_FLA_Wong_DrillsAreOUT.pdf)
- Spacing, meta-analysis: Kim & Webb (2022), Language Learning 72(1), 37 experiments. Confirms spaced practice beats massed overall, with different effects by language area, learner and activity. Some subgroup results are inconclusive because few studies exist — [Serrano 2022 review, SSLLT 12(3)](https://dx.doi.org/10.14746/ssllt.2022.12.3.2)
- Lag (gap length) for grammar is inconsistent. From Serrano's (2022) review — [SSLLT](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/35602):
  - Bird (2010), adult EFL, English tenses: 3-day vs 14-day spacing. No difference at 7 days; 14-day spacing better at 60 days.
  - Rogers (2015), university students: 7-day spacing beat 2.25-day for hard structures.
  - Kasprowicz et al. (2019), children learning French morphology: 3.5 vs 7 days, no difference.
  - Suzuki & DeKeyser (2017), adults learning Japanese morphology: 1 vs 7 days. Accuracy the same; 1-day spacing gave faster performance at 28 days.
  - Suzuki (2017): shorter lag (3.3 vs 7 days) better for production accuracy. Suzuki (2018): short lag better for automatisation (lower coefficient of variation).
  - Learners with higher grammar-inferencing ability benefited more from long (7-day) spacing (Suzuki & DeKeyser 2017b; Suzuki 2019).
- Interleaving: Nakata & Suzuki (2019), MLJ 103(3): mixed grammar practice (English structures) showed no immediate difference from blocked practice. But it was significantly better on a 7-day delayed test — [Serrano 2022](https://dx.doi.org/10.14746/ssllt.2022.12.3.2); [citation details](https://sciences-du-langage.univ-tlse2.fr/accueil/manifestations/seminaire-lnpl-interleaving-for-l2-grammar-learning-is-mixing-structures-beneficial-maud-pelissier-altae-urp-3967-universite-paris-cite)
- Interleaving replications are mixed. Suzuki & Sunada (2020) found blocked-then-interleaved (hybrid) best for relative pronouns. Suzuki et al. (2022b) found interleaving helped immediate oral production but not at 7 days. Suzuki (2021) found blocked task repetition gave more fluent speech — [Serrano 2022](https://dx.doi.org/10.14746/ssllt.2022.12.3.2)

### Inferences
- An SRS queue of grammar exercises is consistent with the spacing evidence. For grammar, don't let intervals grow as aggressively as for vocabulary early on. Short gaps (1–4 days) look at least as good for accurate, fast production during initial learning. Longer gaps can follow once the item is stable.
- Use a hybrid schedule. Do a short block of one topic right after the explanation. Then mix that topic's items into the general queue with other topics. Mixing forces the learner to pick the rule (e.g. Akkusativ vs Dativ after a two-way preposition), not just repeat it.
- Weight output (transformation, word order, write-the-form) over recognition (multiple choice), because the target is writing. Keep some input items, where the learner must read for meaning to choose (e.g. "who did what to whom" from case endings). Learners often ignore German case cues (see section 7).
- Make drills meaningful where possible. The learner should need to understand the sentence to answer, e.g. the answer depends on context, not just the cue word. Pure pattern-substitution items are the weakest-supported type.

### Gaps
- No German-specific spacing or interleaving grammar study found.
- Kim & Webb (2022) pooled effect sizes and grammar-specific subgroup results not retrieved (paywall).
- Processing instruction effect sizes (VanPatten; Shintani 2015) not retrieved.
- No evidence found on gap-fill vs multiple choice vs transformation formats compared directly.

## 3. Corrective feedback (oral and written)

### Takeaway
Corrective feedback works, with medium effects that last over time. For written feedback, the newest and largest synthesis (52 studies) finds moderate, durable effects. Direct, indirect and metalinguistic feedback give similar sizes. Proficiency is the strongest moderator. Lower-proficiency learners benefit more from direct correction (being shown the right form).

### Cited Findings
- Li (2010), Language Learning 60(2), 33 studies (22 published, 11 dissertations): medium overall effect, maintained over time. Explicit feedback had larger immediate effects. Implicit feedback effects were better maintained. Lab studies > classroom; shorter treatments > longer; foreign-language contexts > second-language contexts — [search summary of Li 2010](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/24985?show=full); [CASLS InterCom](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/22384)
- Lyster & Saito (2010), 15 classroom studies (N = 827): oral CF had significant and durable effects on target-language development. Moderators examined: CF type, outcome measure and timing, setting, treatment length, age — [Cambridge SSLA abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)
- Mackey & Goo (2007, interaction meta-analysis), as reported second-hand: CF in interaction d = 0.71 immediate, 1.09 delayed — [search summary; secondary](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b)
- Kang & Han (2015), MLJ 99(1), 21 studies, written CF: small-to-moderate effect on written accuracy. More effective for intermediate and advanced learners. For lower-proficiency learners, direct correction better than indirect. Composition-type writing more responsive. Focused feedback not confirmed as superior — [ELT Research Bites via CASLS](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/24045); [Scinapse record](https://scinapse.io/papers/2005247852)
- Lim & Renandya (2020), 35 studies, ten moderators: moderate overall effect of written CF on grammatical accuracy. Direct feedback had a larger effect than indirect, but not significantly so. Learner proficiency was the strongest moderator — [NIE repository](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/handle/10497/22307)
- Brown, Liu & Norouzian (2023), Bayesian meta-analysis, 52 studies, Language Teaching Research: "robust evidence of the durability of moderate effectiveness of WCF over time." Direct, indirect and metalinguistic feedback all gave similar effect sizes — [todoele summary](https://todoele.net/node/271041)
- Focused vs unfocused: focused feedback (only one or a few error types) has often outperformed unfocused feedback. Critics say it is unrealistic in real classrooms — [Cambridge/abjadia summary](https://ejournal.uin-malang.ac.id/index.php/abjadia/article/view/5364/0). Van Beuningen, De Jong & Kuiken (2012, Language Learning): comprehensive (all errors) direct and indirect CF both gave sustained accuracy gains — [publinova record](https://publinova.nl/product/the-effectiveness-of-comprehensive-corrective-feedback-in-improving-l2-written-accuracy). An approximate replication of Bitchener & Knoch (2010a) and Van Beuningen et al. (2012) exists — [Univ. of Melbourne](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/985272-written-corrective-feedback-studies--approximate-replication-of-bitchener-&-knoch-(2010a)-and-van-beuningen--de-jong-&-kuiken-(2012))

### Inferences
- Exercises: give immediate, explicit feedback with the correct form and a one-line rule ("dative after *mit*"). Explicit feedback has larger immediate effects, and the learner has no teacher. Later re-tests through SRS supply the "durability" check.
- Direct feedback suits an A2/B1 learner. Indirect cues ("error here, find it") need knowledge the learner may lack. As the learner nears B2/C1, the app could show an error *location* or *category* first and reveal the correction on request. This is an inference from proficiency moderators, not a tested app design.
- Focus: the learner can't fix everything. Let each writing task carry 1–3 target structures (from current grammar topics). Ask the learner to check those first. This approximates focused feedback without a marker.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled effect sizes for Li (2010), Lyster & Saito (2010), Kang & Han (2015), Lim & Renandya (2020) not verified (paywalls). Commonly quoted values (Li d ≈ 0.61; Lyster & Saito d ≈ 0.74; Kang & Han g ≈ 0.68) are unverified here.
- Almost all written CF studies use teacher-provided feedback. Self-generated feedback against a model has much less evidence (see section 4).

## 4. Self-correction without a teacher: model texts, reformulation, error logs, checklists

### Takeaway
Comparing one's own draft with a model text is a researched form of written feedback. Learners notice mainly vocabulary and chunks (60–92% of what they notice). Grammar is noticed far less. Guided noticing (prompts telling learners what to look for) and higher proficiency increase what learners notice. Gains held 1–2 weeks in recent controlled studies. Evidence on error logs and checklists in self-study was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Hanaoka (2007), Language Teaching Research, 37 Japanese university EFL learners, four stages: (1) write and note problems; (2) compare with native-speaker model texts and note differences; (3) rewrite; (4) rewrite again two months later. Learners noticed solutions in the models and used them in revisions, predominantly lexical features. No control group — [Wu, Qie & Wang 2023 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1156553/pdf). A search summary gave 92.4% of noticed features as lexical; not verified in the full text, treat as unconfirmed — [search result set incl. Asian EFL thesis](https://mail.asian-efl-journal.com/Thesis_M_Abe.pdf)
- Martínez Esteban & Roca de Larios (2010), 17 Spanish secondary learners: mostly lexical problems noticed. Learners often could not find solutions because models were not adapted to their level. No control group — [Wu et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1156553/pdf)
- Cánovas Guirao et al. (2015), children with control group: models drew attention to lexis and chunks rather than grammar. Noticing and uptake depended on proficiency — [Wu et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1156553/pdf)
- Coyle & Roca de Larios (2014, p. 453) define model texts as native-like texts adapted to "the learners' age and proficiency level as well as to the content and genre of the writing task at hand". Learners use them to explore alternative words, structures and ideas — [quoted in Wu et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1156553/pdf); related work on models as feedback with young learners — [Cánovas Guirao thesis](https://digitum.um.es/digitum/bitstream/10201/55971/1/Tesis_Josefa_C%c3%a1novas_Girao.pdf); [Coyle & Cánovas Guirao, CLIL journal](https://revistes.uab.cat/clil/article/view/22)
- García Mayo & Loidi Labandibar (2017, ARAL), 60 Basque-Spanish teenagers: mainly lexical noticing, plus content. More proficient and *guided* learners noticed more. Learners with more positive beliefs about modelling incorporated more — [Cambridge ARAL abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-review-of-applied-linguistics/article/use-of-models-as-written-corrective-feedback-in-english-as-a-foreign-language-eflwriting/4ED84FB321C3C44107E6A6FE1C992802)
- Ramón (2019), 30 secondary students: a group that reported features *and* explained and rehearsed them vs report-only. Both mainly noticed lexis and ideas; final writing improved in lexis, form and ideas — [Wu et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1156553/pdf)
- Kang (2020), 40 high-school students, argumentative task: models improved lexis and content. Text type affected the benefit. Kang (2022), 67 college learners: model-text group improved significantly and kept gains 2 weeks later. Working memory predicted benefit — [Wu et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1156553/pdf)
- Wu, Qie & Wang (2023), 60 Chinese EFL learners, four-stage argumentative task: 60.6% of noticed features were lexical (71.9% in the comparison stage). Higher language-analytic-ability and guided-noticing learners got more from models. Models improved lexis, grammar, content and organisation; benefit held after 1 week — [Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1156553/pdf)
- Methodological limits flagged in the literature: most studies use short picture-story tasks (which push attention to vocabulary), count incorporated items rather than measure quality, and lack delayed tests. So the evidence mostly shows *uptake*, not long-term acquisition — [Wu et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1156553/pdf)
- Hanaoka's noticing framework (learners compare their output with a reformulated or model text) has been widely replicated for both reformulations and models — [search summary](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A236550)

### Inferences
- The app's self-compare step is evidence-based, but unguided comparison will mostly capture vocabulary. Grammar errors will slip through. Add a short guided comparison checklist tied to the task's target structures. Examples: "Verb in position 2 in main clauses? Verb at the end after *weil/dass*? Case after each preposition? Adjective endings?" The guidance effect (García Mayo & Loidi Labandibar; Wu et al.) supports this.
- Require the learner to note a problem *before* seeing the model (Hanaoka stage 1: "I didn't know how to say X"). This primes noticing and is cheap to implement (a notes field shown before the model is revealed).
- Add a rewrite step after comparison, and a delayed rewrite (Hanaoka stage 4) scheduled through SRS days or weeks later. The rewrite is where uptake is measured and practised.
- Model answers should be at or slightly above the learner's level (i+1), not C2 prose. Learners could not use non-adapted models (Martínez Esteban & Roca de Larios). Consider two models per task (e.g. a B1 version and a B2 version).
- Logged mistakes becoming review cards is consistent with focused feedback plus spaced retrieval. No direct study of self-logged error cards was found. Cards should present the learner's own wrong sentence for correction, not just the rule. That keeps the practice output-based and context-bound.
- Learners' own error detection is limited by their knowledge. Self-comparison will miss errors in structures the learner hasn't been taught. Linking the checklist to grammar topics already studied is a sensible constraint.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests error logs or self-editing checklists in unsupervised adult self-study.
- No study found comparing model-text comparison with teacher correction head-to-head with effect sizes.
- No DaF-specific model-text studies found.
- Reformulation studies (e.g. Qi & Lapkin 2001; Adams 2003) not retrieved.

## 5. Dictation and dictogloss

### Takeaway
Evidence for dictogloss (hear a short text, then reconstruct it in writing) is mostly small classroom studies with mixed grammar results and positive learner attitudes. No meta-analysis was found. Treat it as plausible, weakly evidenced.

### Cited Findings
- Dictogloss directs attention to form and function as learners reconstruct a spoken text, traditionally in groups — [search summary of dictogloss literature](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/books/9789027260840-lllt.55.01cal)
- Mixed results: one study found no significant difference in verb-tense performance between dictogloss and comparison groups, though most students liked the task. Others report accuracy gains on written tests. Advanced-low learners received it better — [search summaries; single studies](https://repositorio.usfq.edu.ec/bitstream/23000/12056/1/215435.pdf); [Revistia EJLS](https://revistia.com/ejls/article/view/987); [SSLLT 2014 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/043a72c89a83407eb185afb34c32056f)
- Verbalising one's understanding during dictogloss can act like peer negotiation (single-study claim) — [search summary](https://doaj.org/article/043a72c89a83407eb185afb34c32056f)

### Inferences
- A solo dictogloss fits the app without marking: play audio (e.g. the model answer read aloud), learner reconstructs, then compares word-by-word with the transcript. The comparison is objective, so self-marking works better than for free writing. Differences are mostly form (endings, word order), which partly fixes the "models only teach vocabulary" problem.
- Use texts dense in the current grammar target. Keep the evidence label modest.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of dictation or dictogloss located. Most studies are small, classroom-based and group-based. Solo, computer-based dictogloss is untested in what I found.
- No DaF dictation research found.

## 6. Sentence-combining, translation and transformation drills

### Takeaway
Sentence combining has L1 meta-analytic support (Graham & Perin 2007 lists it among 11 effective practices for adolescent writers). Small L2 studies show gains in syntactic complexity, mainly coordination. No L2 meta-analysis was found. Translation and transformation drills lack strong evidence for writing development.

### Cited Findings
- Graham & Perin (2007), "Writing Next" (L1 adolescents), meta-analysis: sentence combining and study of models both among the 11 effective practices. Others include writing strategies, summarisation, specific product goals, prewriting and process writing — [AdLit summary](https://www.adlit.org/topics/writing/summary-writing-next)
- L2: a mixed-methods study found the sentence-combining group gained more in syntactic complexity than other groups, mainly in coordination, less in subordination — [AILA 2021 abstract](https://aila2021.dryfta.com/program/abstract-archive/abstract/public/1037/a-mixed-methods-approach-to-understanding-the-development-of-syntactic-complexity-in-l2-writing); [Temple dissertation](https://scholarshare.temple.edu/handle/20.500.12613/1835); [JALT](https://jalt-publications.org/node/4/articles/5414-developing-syntactic-complexity-l2-writing)
- EFL undergraduates: sentence-combining instruction significantly improved three dimensions of syntactic complexity (single study) — [IJED](https://ojs.mahadewa.ac.id/index.php/ijed/article/view/4802)

### Inferences
- Sentence combining is well suited to German, because combining forces word-order choices. Examples: two main clauses to a *weil/obwohl/dass* clause (verb-final); relative clauses (case of relative pronoun); infinitive clauses with *um…zu*. It is self-markable: give a target and accept a small set of answers, or show a model and self-compare.
- Complexity for C1 (subordination, nominal style, participial attributes) won't come from free writing alone. Sentence-combining tasks are a targeted bridge from B1 to C1 syntax.
- Transformation drills (active to passive, direct to reported speech) are a form of output practice. They are supported to the extent output practice is (section 2), but not shown to improve free writing on their own.

### Gaps
- No L2 meta-analysis on sentence combining found. Graham & Perin's effect sizes not retrieved, and it is L1 research.
- No good evidence found on translation exercises for L2 writing development.
- No direct evidence found on transformation drills and free writing quality.

## 7. Order of acquisition in German and topic sequencing A2→C1

### Takeaway
German verb placement develops in a fixed, implicational sequence (Processability Theory). The sequence was confirmed with a 100% fit in a study of tutored English-speaking learners. Teachability Hypothesis: instruction can't make learners skip stages. Case is hard: accusative before dative; one-case prepositions before two-way. Intermediate adult learners often ignore case cues in comprehension.

### Cited Findings
- Processability Theory and the Teachability Hypothesis grew from research on German L2 word order. Verb placement is the central developmental marker (Pienemann 1989) — [search summary](https://sapporo-u.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/7664/files/SULB00001036.pdf); [FAU "Every verb in its right place?" roadmap](https://cris.fau.de/publications/358965842)
- Teachability Hypothesis: teaching a structure is limited by the same processing constraints that set natural developmental sequences — [search summary](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/items/6fb5de9d-737f-48af-bc83-099d523d0aff)
- Cross-sectional study, 21 tutored English-L1 learners of German (spontaneous data): implicational scale confirmed PT's word-order predictions with 100% fit. Order and cumulativeness supported; strict sequential nature less clear — [ANU research portal](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/acquisition-of-german-word-order-in-tutored-learners-a-cross-sect/)
- Subordinate clause development in German and Swedish L2 studied separately (SSLA) — [Cambridge SSLA](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/development-of-subordinate-clauses-in-german-and-swedish-as-l2s/93EBE17756E01E6F9DC7895410AFEA5F)
- Case (child L2/bilingual data): accusative more accurate than dative. Dative most difficult; learners overgeneralise accusative into dative contexts. One-case prepositions before two-way prepositions. Comprehension precedes production — [Linguistic Approaches to Bilingualism](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/lia.00005.int); [jbe-platform](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/lia.00006.int)
- Adult intermediate L2 German learners (year-long study) relied on word order and meaning, not case marking, in comprehension. Use of case cues improved with repeated exposure — [Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-use-and-non-use-of-semantic-information-word-order-and-case-m/)

### Inferences
- Sequence word order roughly as PT predicts: canonical SVO, adverb fronting, separated verb parts (modal + infinitive, separable prefixes, Perfekt), subject-verb inversion after fronted elements, then verb-final in subordinate clauses. An A2 learner may still be unstable on inversion and verb-final. B1 work should recycle these constantly, not treat them as "done".
- Teachability doesn't mean "don't teach": explicit instruction (section 1) still helps. It means expect errors in later-stage structures in free writing even after correct gap-fill answers. The app shouldn't treat logged word-order errors as a failure of the topic.
- Case sequence: accusative → dative → two-way prepositions → genitive. Train comprehension of case (who did what to whom when the object is fronted, e.g. *Den Hund sieht der Mann*) as well as production, given evidence that learners ignore case cues.
- C1 topics (Konjunktiv I, extended participial attributes, nominalisation, Passiversatzformen) depend on stable verb placement and case. Place them after the learner is reliable on those in their own writing, judged by error-log rates, not by exercise scores.

### Gaps
- Exact PT stage labels and German stage list (Initial: SVO → ADV → SEP → INV → V-END) not verified from primary sources here. This is the commonly cited sequence from Pienemann / ZISA research; treat as unverified.
- No adult-specific case-acquisition-order study with effect sizes retrieved. The case findings above come largely from child bilingual data.
- No evidence found on acquisition order for adjective endings, Konjunktiv, or passive in adult L2 German.

## 8. Writing volume, frequency and genre

### Takeaway
I found no robust evidence quantifying how much writing produces how much development in adult L2. Genre-based practice matching the target exam is supported mainly by test alignment, not by experimental evidence. Goethe C1 writing requires a forum post giving and justifying an opinion and a formal message (75 minutes).

### Cited Findings
- Goethe-Zertifikat C1 Schreiben: express and justify an opinion in a forum post on a social topic, and write a formal message; 75 minutes — [Goethe-Institut](https://www.goethe.de/ins/ph/en/m/spr/prf/gzc1/w24.html); [C1 handbook (PDF)](https://goethe.de/pro/relaunch/prf/bg/Handbuch_Pruefungsziele_Testbeschreibung_C1.pdf)
- Goethe B1 sample papers (adult) — [B1 Übungssatz Erwachsene (PDF)](https://www.goethe.de/pro/relaunch/prf/materialien/B1/B1_Uebungssatz_Erwachsene.pdf)
- L1 (Graham & Perin 2007): study of models, specific product goals and prewriting are effective practices — [AdLit](https://www.adlit.org/topics/writing/summary-writing-next)
- Model-text effects vary by text type; argumentative tasks prompt attention to content and organisation as well as lexis — [Wu et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1156553/pdf)
- Written CF was more effective in composition-type writing (Kang & Han 2015) — [CASLS](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/24045)

### Inferences
- Sequence genres by level, aligned to Goethe: informal/semi-formal emails and messages (A2–B1), forum posts giving opinions (B1–B2), formal messages and argumentative forum posts/essays (B2–C1). The existing task structure (content points, useful phrases, model) is a sensible genre scaffold. Content points serve as "specific product goals" (an L1-supported practice).
- Frequent short tasks with the full cycle (plan, write, compare, log, rewrite later) are likely better than rare long ones, given spacing evidence and the need for feedback cycles. This is inference, not tested.
- Useful phrases: expect learners to borrow chunks, as model-text studies show. Check later whether borrowed chunks appear in new texts, e.g. an SRS card asking for the phrase in a new context.

### Gaps
- No study found that quantifies writing volume or frequency against L2 writing development in adults.
- No experimental evidence found on genre-based pedagogy (e.g. Systemic Functional Linguistics–based) for DaF learners; not searched in depth.
- Goethe B1 writing format details (tasks, word counts) not extracted; check the B1 Übungssatz PDF above.
