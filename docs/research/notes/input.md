# Reading and listening input for adult L2 learners (A2 → C1 German self-study app)

Scope note: almost all primary evidence is from EFL/ESL learners (mostly Japanese, Taiwanese, Brazilian, Thai university students). Transfer to adult L1-English learners of German is assumed, not tested. Several sources were reachable only as abstracts or secondary summaries; this is flagged per item.

## 1. Extensive reading (ER) and graded readers: meta-analytic effects; how much reading for incidental vocabulary

### Takeaway
ER reliably improves reading comprehension and especially reading rate (small-to-medium effects; adults benefit more than children), but vocabulary gains per text are small and decay fast. Words met fewer than ~8 times in one book are rarely retained; volume across many texts, plus repetition, is what drives vocabulary growth.

### Cited Findings
- Jeon & Day (2016), *Reading in a Foreign Language* 28(2): 71 samples, 49 studies (1980–2014), 5,919 participants; "small to medium" effects for both experimental-vs-control and pre-post designs. Moderators: larger effects for adults than children/adolescents; EFL > ESL; web-based stories > paper books; ER integrated into the curriculum gave the highest effects — [ScholarSpace abstract](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/b52a5591-ec04-4026-816f-434361a4e899/full)
- Jeon & Day (2016) by age: adults d = 0.70, children d = 0.52, adolescents d = 0.35 (secondary summary) — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188915)
- Nakanishi (2015), *TESOL Quarterly*: large effect on reading rate (d = 0.98), medium on reading comprehension (d = 0.63), small on vocabulary (d = 0.18); longer programmes and age were examined as moderators (secondary summary) — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188915); [ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1078389.pdf)
- Waring & Takaki (2003): one 400-headword graded reader; 25 target words in frequency bands from 1 to 15–18 occurrences; tests immediately, after 1 week, after 3 months. On average, the meaning of only 1 of 25 words was remembered after 3 months; none of the words met fewer than 8 times were remembered. Frequent words were learned more and decayed less. Authors' conclusion: "a massive amount of graded reading is needed to build new vocabulary" (single study) — [Waring & Takaki 2003 PDF](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/1626146/Waring-Takaki-2003.pdf); [RFL record](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/78)
- Webb & Chang (2015), *Language Teaching Research* 19(6): learners read-while-listening to 10 graded readers over an ER programme; relative vocabulary gains 44.06% immediately and 36.66% three months later. Relationships between learning and frequency/distribution of occurrence were non-significant — frequency was one factor among many in a long programme — [UWO repository](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/edupub/94)
- Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019) meta-analysis (*Language Learning*), 26 studies across reading, listening, reading-while-listening and viewing: correlation between frequency of occurrence and incidental learning r = .34 (medium) (secondary summary) — [Secondary citation, EIP 2023](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/eip-2023-0001.pdf)
- Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa (2023) meta-analysis "How effective is second language incidental vocabulary learning?": 24 studies, 29 effect sizes, N = 2,771. Large overall effects vs controls, but mean proportion of target words learned only 9–18% (immediate) and 6–17% (delayed). By mode: reading 17%/15%, listening 15%/13%, reading-while-listening 13%/17%, viewing 7%/5% (immediate/delayed) — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C); [todoele summary](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/how-effective-second-language-incidental-vocabulary-learning-meta-analysis)

### Inferences
- Treat reading in the app as the main engine for reading fluency and comprehension, and as a slow, high-volume source of vocabulary. Do not expect one text with a glossary to teach its glossed words.
- Recycle target words across many texts. A word that appears once in one text has close to zero chance of long-term retention from reading alone. Track per-word encounter counts across the corpus and aim for 8–10+ meetings, spread over time.
- Since Webb & Chang found high gains over 10 books, the unit that matters is a series of texts, not one text. Design for sustained, frequent sessions (adults showed the largest ER effects).
- Pair reading with deliberate review (e.g. spaced flashcards of glossed words), because incidental uptake per text is 10–20% and decays.

### Gaps
- Exact overall d values for Jeon & Day (between-group and pre-post) were not visible in the abstract I could access; the commonly quoted figures (≈0.57 and ≈0.79) are from memory and unverified here.
- No German-specific ER meta-analysis found. German inflection and compounding may change the "encounters needed" figure (lemma vs inflected form); no evidence located.
- Nation's word-count targets for ER (e.g. words read per week) were not retrieved.

## 2. Intensive vs extensive reading balance; optimal text difficulty (known-word coverage)

### Takeaway
Comprehension rises steeply with lexical coverage. 95% known words is a minimum for supported reading; 98% gives adequate independent comprehension. ER material should sit at ~98%+; intensive, glossed study texts can sit nearer 95%.

### Cited Findings
- Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010): optimal lexical coverage 98% (≈8,000 word families in English) for adequate comprehension; minimal coverage 95% (≈4,000–5,000 families) for "readers with support". A 0.8% coverage gain (5,000→6,000 level) went with the largest jump in comprehension. Required coverage depends on the degree of comprehension wanted — [Secondary summary, Ritsumeikan repository](https://ritsumei.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/16831/files/3.pdf); [RFL 2010 full text](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/67407/1/33_2_10125-67407.pdf)
- Graded readers: Waring & Takaki's text was a 400-headword level reader, and even at that level most new words were not retained — [Waring & Takaki 2003](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/1626146/Waring-Takaki-2003.pdf)
- Coverage thresholds for viewing differ from reading; a 2024 SSLA study compared coverage needs in L1 and L2 viewing (not reviewed in detail) — [Cambridge Core, SSLA 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/DFCA6605076705D5762C98F286D16B27)

### Inferences
- Compute coverage per text against the learner's known-word list (lemmatised German) and label texts: ≥98% = "fluent/extensive" mode (no or minimal glosses, read fast, few questions); 95–98% = "study/intensive" mode (glossary, comprehension questions); <95% = too hard for this learner now.
- At A2→B1 most volume should be easy, high-coverage text; intensive texts are the minority used to introduce new vocabulary and grammar. As the known-word list grows, the same texts move from intensive to extensive band — a natural re-reading schedule.
- Gloss enough unknown words to lift effective coverage towards 98% in intensive texts.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis located that directly compares intensive vs extensive reading proportions in a self-study setting. The "mostly extensive, some intensive" split is practitioner consensus (Nation's four strands), not tested here.
- Hu & Nation (2000) (98% for unassisted fiction) and Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011) (linear coverage–comprehension relation) were not retrieved in this session; cite from originals if used.
- German-specific coverage studies (word families vs lemmas, compounds) not found.

## 3. Glossing: L1 vs L2, marginal vs pop-up, effect on incidental vocabulary

### Takeaway
Glosses roughly add 15–20 percentage points to incidental word learning. L1 glosses beat L2 glosses at all proficiency levels. Glosses that make the learner choose (multiple-choice glosses) work best; a separate glossary list or in-text glosses work least well. Mode (text, picture, audio) does not matter much.

### Cited Findings
- Yanagisawa, Webb & Uchihara (2020), *SSLA* 42(2): meta-regression, 42 studies, 359 effect sizes, N = 3,802. Glossed reading: 45.3% (immediate) and 33.4% (delayed) of target words learned vs 26.6% and 19.8% for non-glossed reading — [Cambridge Core](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922)
- Same study: multiple-choice glosses most effective; in-text glosses and glossaries least effective; L1 glosses > L2 glosses; no interaction between gloss language and proficiency (beginner/intermediate/advanced); no significant difference between textual, pictorial and auditory glosses — [Cambridge Core](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922)
- Chang (2019, SSLA, narrow reading/listening study): glossing did not interact with time, while other factors did (single study) — [Cambridge Core abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/effects-of-narrow-reading-and-listening-on-l2-vocabulary-learning/636D23C4D711F7E93779D98BF7F16F8B)

### Inferences
- Use English (L1) glosses through C1, not German definitions; the meta-analysis found no proficiency level where L2 glosses won.
- An end-of-text glossary is the weakest form. Prefer glosses attached to the word in context (tap/hover, or margin), and consider a "guess first" multiple-choice gloss for target words: show 2–3 candidate meanings, learner picks, then reveal. This matches the most effective format and adds retrieval effort.
- Audio glosses (tap to hear the word) are fine as an add-on but are not expected to boost learning by themselves.
- Log gloss look-ups; a looked-up word is a candidate for spaced review.

### Gaps
- Pop-up (hyperlinked) vs marginal glosses was not separable from the summary available; the full paper's gloss-type categories need checking.
- Effects of glossing on reading comprehension (as opposed to vocabulary) and on reading speed were not covered.

## 4. Narrow reading and narrow listening

### Takeaway
Reading or listening to many texts on one topic, author or series recycles vocabulary and is theoretically well motivated, but direct comparative evidence is thin: mostly single studies, corpus analyses and small classroom studies. No meta-analysis found.

### Cited Findings
- Chang (2019), *SSLA*: learners read-and-listened to three graded readers of one title (*The Railway Children*) or three Sherlock Holmes books; 60 target words; gains in written and aural meaning were more likely than gains in spelling and use; retention measured at five weeks. All participants did narrow input, so no narrow-vs-non-narrow comparison (single study) — [Cambridge Core abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/effects-of-narrow-reading-and-listening-on-l2-vocabulary-learning/636D23C4D711F7E93779D98BF7F16F8B)
- Corpus-driven work shows that thematically related texts recycle vocabulary more than unrelated texts; small classroom studies report better retention for narrow readers (secondary review, mixed-quality sources) — [LEARN Journal review](https://so04.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/LEARN/article/view/282350)
- Waring & Takaki (2003) and Uchihara et al. (2019) show retention depends on frequency of meeting a word, which is the mechanism narrow input exploits — [Waring & Takaki 2003](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/1626146/Waring-Takaki-2003.pdf); [Secondary citation of Uchihara et al.](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/eip-2023-0001.pdf)

### Inferences
- Organise content into topic clusters or recurring-character series (e.g. 6–10 texts per theme: Wohnung, Arbeit, Gesundheit), with reading and listening texts sharing vocabulary. This is a cheap way to hit the 8+ encounter threshold.
- Topic familiarity also lowers comprehension load, which lets learners handle slightly harder texts later in a cluster.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that cleanly compares narrow vs varied input for adult learners on comprehension or vocabulary. Krashen's original narrow reading/listening proposals are advocacy, not experiments.

## 5. Reading-while-listening (RWL); listening with/without transcripts or captions; metacognitive listening

### Takeaway
RWL is at least as good as reading alone for vocabulary and is better than listening alone for long-term retention in some studies. Captions/transcripts reliably raise comprehension and vocabulary learning from audiovisual input (meta-analytic g ≈ .6–.9). But captions support comprehension of that text; they do not by themselves train decoding of unscripted speech. Metacognitive listening instruction helps, especially weaker listeners.

### Cited Findings
- Brown, Waring & Donkaewbua (2008), *RFL* 20(2): reading, RWL and listening to graded-reader stories; words learned in all modes but most not learned; at 3 months unprompted recall kept on average 1 of 28 words in either reading mode and 0 of 28 in listening-only (single study) — [RFL 2008](https://www2.hawaii.edu/~readfl/rfl/October2008/brown/brown.html); [PDF](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/66816/1/20_2_10125_66816_brown.pdf)
- Webb et al. (2023) meta-analysis: incidental gains similar across reading (17%/15%), listening (15%/13%) and RWL (13%/17%); viewing lower (7%/5%) — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C)
- Webb & Chang (2015): RWL across 10 graded readers produced 44% relative vocabulary gains, 37% at 3 months — [UWO repository](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/edupub/94)
- Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate & Desmet (2013), *System* 41(3): meta-analysis of 18 studies on captioned video; large effect on vocabulary learning (g = .87) — [Secondary citation, NTUST thesis](https://etheses.lib.ntust.edu.tw/thesis/detail/d904414821b7e2657cea7976af8dd6cb/?seq=6); [Secondary citation](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/f7d7879b-ed0c-46e4-a87f-ee782f8b47bf/full)
- A separate, larger meta-analysis (32 studies, >2,389 participants, random-effects) found a moderate effect of captions on listening comprehension (g = .61) — [Secondary citation, NTUST thesis](https://etheses.lib.ntust.edu.tw/thesis/detail/d904414821b7e2657cea7976af8dd6cb/?seq=6)
- Keyword vs full captions for listening comprehension is an active research question (ReCALL study "Is less more?") — [Cambridge Core, ReCALL](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/is-less-more-effectiveness-and-perceived-usefulness-of-keyword-and-full-captioned-video-for-l2-listening-comprehension/9AD29F86C1752148FDBB500921922223)
- Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari (2010), *Language Learning*: 106 university learners of French; the group taught a metacognitive listening cycle (predict → listen → verify → discuss → listen again → reflect) significantly outperformed a control group on comprehension; gains largest for less-skilled listeners (single study; later replicated approximately) — [Learning to Listen summary](https://agpci.dlsu.edu.ph/agpci/article.php?a=22472); [Replication study, Language Teaching](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/replication-research-in-l2-listening-comprehension-a-conceptual-replication-of-graham-macaro-2008-and-an-approximate-replication-of-vandergrift-tafaghodtari-2010-and-brett-1997/95952E2358299C05E78A66145F13ABE4)

### Inferences
- Offer three listening modes per script: listen-only, listen-with-transcript (RWL), and transcript after listening. Default sequence for intermediate learners: listen first without text (attempt + questions), then RWL to resolve gaps, then listen again without text. This mirrors the metacognitive cycle and keeps the decoding challenge while still giving the vocabulary/comprehension benefit of text support.
- Do not make the transcript the default first exposure; the caption evidence shows it boosts comprehension of the item, but the listening skill being trained is decoding speech without text.
- Add light metacognitive prompts: a prediction step before listening ("What words do you expect?") and a short reflection after ("Which part was hard: speed, unknown words, linking?"). Cheap to build; evidence favours it for weaker listeners.
- Use RWL as the default for extensive reading of easier texts: same vocabulary benefit, extra form–sound mapping for German spelling-to-sound.

### Gaps
- The exact listening-comprehension effect size from Montero Perez et al. (2013) could not be verified (publisher page returned 403). It is often quoted as g ≈ 0.99 (large); treat as unverified. Note most caption studies use video, not audio-only with transcript.
- Montero Perez's later reviews (e.g. 2022 *Language Teaching*) and Field's *Listening in the Language Classroom* (2008) — which argues for teaching decoding sub-skills rather than only testing comprehension — were not retrieved; their content here is not evidenced.
- No study found isolating RWL with TTS audio.

## 6. Listening speed and repeated listening

### Takeaway
Repetition is one of the more useful listening supports, but helps higher-proficiency learners more. Evidence on slowed speech is mixed and mostly from single studies; modest slowing may help comprehension, but extreme slowing does not, and exposure to natural rate is needed for skill transfer.

### Cited Findings
- Chang & Read (2006), *TESOL Quarterly* 40(2), 375–397: compared four listening supports — previewing questions, repeated input, background information, vocabulary instruction. Previewing questions had the strongest effect on strategy use; repetition appeared more beneficial for higher-proficiency listeners because low-proficiency listeners lack the linguistic resources to exploit a second hearing (secondary summaries) — [SOAR repository paper citing Chang & Read](https://soar-ir.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2283/files/EducationR06-02.pdf); [Pearson, "Listen once or twice"](https://www.pearsonpte.com/content/dam/ELL/pte/pearsonpte/general/Listen-Once-Or-Twice--Glyn-Jones.pdf)
- Metacognitive cycle in Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari (2010) includes multiple listenings with verification between them — [Learning to Listen summary](https://agpci.dlsu.edu.ph/agpci/article.php?a=22472)

### Inferences
- Default to at least two listenings with a task between them (questions previewed before the first). Encourage a third listen after reading the transcript.
- Offer playback speeds of roughly 0.85×–1.0×, not extreme slow-down. Since Piper allows synthesis at a set rate, generate audio at natural rate and let the player slow it, rather than making slow audio the default. Progress learners towards 1.0× by B1–B2.
- At A2/B1, pair repetition with support (pre-taught key words, previewed questions), since repetition alone helps weaker listeners less.

### Gaps
- Primary speech-rate studies were not identified reliably. Search returned unattributed claims (e.g. modest slowing to ~98 wpm helping but ~58 wpm not; natural-rate exposure producing larger gains than slow-only); original sources not confirmed, so these are not cited as findings.
- No meta-analysis found on number of repetitions vs gains.

## 7. Synthetic (TTS) vs human speech for L2 listening; speaker variety and natural features

### Takeaway
Small studies find modern TTS about as comprehensible as a human voice for L2 learners on scripted material, though rated less natural. No study found on Piper or on German TTS for learners. TTS from one or two voices lacks talker variability and spontaneous-speech features (reductions, hesitations), which are what learners must eventually decode; talker variability is a documented moderator of perceptual learning.

### Cited Findings
- Bione & Cardoso (2020), *Language Learning & Technology*: 29 Brazilian EFL learners heard stories and sentences in a TTS and a human voice. Comprehensibility ratings similar; naturalness ratings lower for TTS. On text comprehension, dictation and aural identification of past *-ed*, performance was "relatively similar" for both voices (single small study) — [LLT](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/944/)
- Hirai & Omaki (JACET Journal): natural speech tended to be understood better than synthetic speech, but prior exposure to synthetic speech improved its comprehensibility, and synthetic speech was rated almost as natural as human speech by higher and lower proficiency groups (single study) — [Tsukuba repository PDF](https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/48586/files/JacetJ_53_1.pdf)
- Charpentier-Jiménez (2023): Costa Rican ESL students generally preferred human voices but some could not reliably tell them apart (perception study) — [Revista Comunicación](https://revistas.tec.ac.cr/index.php/comunicacion/article/download/6988/6769/24025)
- High-variability phonetic training meta-analysis (79 studies, SSLA 2025): g = 0.92 (pre-post) and g = 0.67 (treatment vs control) for L2 speech perception; effects moderated by number of talkers, training time and task, with retention and some generalisation to new stimuli — [Cambridge Core, SSLA HVPT meta-analysis](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/6ABB8C1F32D88D53EA8D05A4565E76F6/S0272263125100879a.pdf/high-variability-phonetic-training-hvpt-a-meta-analysis-of-l2-perceptual-training-studies.pdf)
- Multi-talker vs lower-variability training is still being tested (2025 *Applied Psycholinguistics*); benefits of many talkers vs few are not settled — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/comparing-lower-and-higher-variability-multitalker-perceptual-training/2755FEDEFB74EC4A3DD83011015BCB97)

### Inferences
- Piper TTS is defensible for comprehension practice and RWL on scripted texts at A2–B1. It is weaker preparation for real German: one clean voice, no regional variety, no fillers (äh, also), no casual reductions (*haste*, *'n*, *ham wa*), no overlap.
- Use several Piper German voices (male/female, different models) and rotate them across texts, to add some talker variability.
- From B1 upward, schedule authentic human audio (e.g. public-broadcaster slow-news and learner podcasts, then normal-speed media) alongside TTS. By B2–C1, most listening should be human, unscripted speech.
- Consider writing dialogue scripts with spoken-German features (Modalpartikeln, contractions, ellipsis) so TTS at least carries the lexical and syntactic features, even if prosody and phonetic reduction are missing.
- Keep a quality check: TTS mispronunciations (stress on compounds, foreign names, abbreviations) teach wrong forms. Studies found TTS adequate, not error-free.

### Gaps
- No study found on Piper or on German neural TTS for learners, or on learning outcomes (as opposed to one-off comprehension) from long-term TTS-only listening.
- No study found measuring whether TTS-trained listeners transfer to spontaneous human speech.
- Evidence on teaching reduced forms/connected speech explicitly (e.g. Field's decoding work) was not retrieved.

## 8. Comprehension questions vs other post-reading tasks (summarising, written retelling)

### Takeaway
Tasks that make learners use or evaluate target words (higher "involvement": need, search, evaluation) produce more incidental vocabulary learning than reading with comprehension questions alone. A 2021 meta-analysis refined this model. Direct evidence that written summaries beat comprehension questions for comprehension or retention was not located.

### Cited Findings
- Yanagisawa & Webb (2021), *SSLA*: meta-analysis of 42 studies testing the Involvement Load Hypothesis (ILH; Laufer & Hulstijn's model where learning depends on need, search and evaluation). Splitting the components into levels and adding other variables explained more variance in gains; the authors produced formulas to predict task effectiveness — [Cambridge Core PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5C5984B326F37FBF3A3DDA3C0EB2996C/S0272263121000577a.pdf/involvement-load-hypothesis-plus-creating-an-improved-predictive-model-of-incidental-vocabulary-learning.pdf)
- Multiple-choice glosses (which require choosing a meaning — a form of evaluation) were the most effective gloss type — [Yanagisawa, Webb & Uchihara 2020](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922)
- Chang & Read (2006): previewing comprehension questions before listening had the largest effect on listener strategy use among the supports tested — [SOAR repository paper](https://soar-ir.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2283/files/EducationR06-02.pdf)

### Inferences
- Keep comprehension questions (they check understanding and, previewed, focus listening), but add at least one higher-involvement task per text: e.g. a 2–3 sentence German summary using 3 target words, or a sentence-writing prompt with a glossed word. Without speaking practice, short writing is the app's main output channel.
- Since the app cannot easily grade free writing, options are: self-check against a model summary, or an LLM-scored check (cost and privacy constraints permitting).
- Make questions answerable only by understanding, not by word-matching, and include some that require inference — otherwise they test scanning.

### Gaps
- The ILH-plus paper's task rankings (e.g. composition writing vs sentence writing vs comprehension questions) could not be extracted (PDF did not parse); check the full text before citing specific rankings.
- No meta-analysis found comparing summarising/retelling with comprehension questions for reading comprehension growth.

## 9. Input volume targets: hours from A2 to B1/B2/C1

### Takeaway
Official figures are guided learning hours (taught class time), not input hours, and sources disagree. Goethe-Institut figures suggest roughly 150–300 extra 45-minute units per level step; FSI puts German at about 36 weeks of full-time study (≈750–900 class hours) for professional working proficiency (roughly B2–C1). Self-study learners should expect B1→C1 to take hundreds of hours beyond A2, with input making up much of it.

### Cited Findings
- Goethe-Institut (via search summary of its course pages): approximately 150 (A1), 300 (A2), 450 (B1), 600 (B2), 750 (C1) teaching units of 45 minutes, apparently cumulative; adult course sub-levels are about 40 units each. Other Goethe pages (and widely circulated figures) give ranges such as A2 150–260, B1 260–490, B2 450–600, C1 600–750 units; I could not open the pages to confirm (403) — [Goethe-Institut London FAQ](https://goethe.de/ins/gb/en/sta/lon/kur/faq.html); [Goethe-Institut Malaysia course levels](https://www.goethe.de/ins/my/en/spr/kon/stu.html)
- FSI: German is Category II, about 36 weeks of full-time instruction; class-hour figures in secondary sources conflict (828 vs 900 hours). Target is ILR 3 "professional working proficiency" for English speakers — [Secondary summary](https://climbtheladder.com/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-german-realistic-timelines/); [Secondary summary](https://legalclarity.org/foreign-service-language-courses-eligibility-and-resources/)
- Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010): learners at 95% coverage needed about 56 more academic hours of instruction to become independent readers (English, single study) — [Secondary summary](https://ritsumei.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/16831/files/3.pdf)

### Inferences
- Using Goethe's cumulative figures, A2→B1 ≈ 150 units (≈110 clock hours), B1→B2 ≈ 150 units, B2→C1 ≈ 150 units: about 340 clock hours from A2 to C1 of guided study. FSI's ≈ 750–900 class hours from zero (plus expected homework) suggests the true total for C1 is higher. Treat C1 as roughly 1–2 years at an hour a day.
- These are classroom hours with teacher feedback. A self-study learner without speaking practice will likely need more, and will develop receptive skills faster than productive ones. CEFR levels for reading/listening may run ahead of overall level.
- Practical app metric: track minutes of reading and listening and words read/heard, rather than hours of "study". Example: at 100–150 words per minute, an hour of reading is 6,000–9,000 words. Webb & Chang's 10-book RWL programme is a useful sense of scale for one term's ER.

### Gaps
- No study found that converts CEFR level steps into hours of comprehensible input specifically. All hour figures are for guided instruction.
- Goethe and Council of Europe primary documents with hour ranges were not accessible (403); the Council of Europe does not itself set official hour figures as far as could be determined.
- FSI's current official page was not retrieved; its exact class-hour figure for German should be checked at state.gov before citing.
